## What is make.fun?

make.fun is a bounty platform for builders. Organizers post competitions
("bounties") with a prize pool, a deadline, and a clear scoring rubric.
You build something — a game, a tool, a forecast, a piece of art, a
tutorial like this one — submit it, and get scored against the rest of the
field. Top entries win cash prizes.

What makes it different from a hackathon:

- **Always on.** New bounties open every day; there is no single weekend
  to block off.
- **Scored, not judged by vibes.** Every competition ships a rubric and,
  where possible, automated checks, so you know exactly what "good" means.
- **Free to enter.** You need an account, a public GitHub repo, and your
  own time. No entry fees, no paid prerequisites.
- **Real payouts.** Prize pools range from a few hundred to a few thousand
  dollars, usually split across the top 10–50 entries.

## Who is this guide for?

- Developers who can ship a small project but have never entered a bounty.
- Students and hobbyists looking for a structured reason to build.
- Anyone who found this repo through a referral link and wants to know
  what the platform actually is before signing up.

No prior bounty experience is assumed. Basic Git and GitHub knowledge helps,
but every step below links to what you need.

approval step — once your account exists, you can browse and enter.

## Step 2 — Connect GitHub

Most make.fun bounties are submitted as a **public GitHub repository**:

1. If you do not have one yet, create a free GitHub account at
   [github.com](https://github.com).
2. In your make.fun account settings, connect GitHub so the platform can
   see your public repos.
3. Keep your submission repo public for the whole competition — private
   repos cannot be scored.

## Step 3 — Pick your first bounty

On the [bounty board](https://make.fun), filter by what you can actually
ship this week:

- **Prize and places paid.** A $1,000 pool paying the top 50 is friendlier
  to a first-timer than a winner-takes-all jackpot.
- **Deadline.** Give yourself real build time. A bounty closing in two
  days is a sprint; one closing in two weeks is a learning project.
- **Scoring rubric.** Read it before you write code. It tells you whether
  the judges value polish, novelty, correctness, or speed — and what the
  automated checks will measure.
- **Starter kit.** Many bounties ship a kit with a CLI, a template, and a
  `check` command. Download it first and run the checks early; a green
  local check is the cheapest way to avoid a rejected submission.

For a first bounty, prefer a small, well-scoped build you can finish over
an ambitious one you cannot. A complete simple entry beats an unfinished
impressive one.

## Step 4 — Build, check, submit

1. **Read the rules.** Every competition page has a `RULES.txt` or rules
   section. Read it fully — including the fine print about repo creation
   dates, file size limits, and what counts as authored work.
2. **Start the session properly.** Some competitions require you to run
   the kit's `init` command before you write your first line, so your
   work is anchored to the competition window. Do it first, not after.
3. **Build in the open.** Commit early and often to your public repo.
   Your commit history is part of the evidence that the work is yours.
4. **Run the checks.** The kit's `check` command mirrors what the server
   will verify. Fix every failure locally before submitting.
5. **Submit from the kit CLI** (usually a `submit` command) and then
   **poll your submission status until it reads `scored`**. A submission
   ID alone is not proof that you passed the gates — only a `scored`
   status means the entry is in.
6. **Watch the leaderboard.** Practice scores and provisional ranks update
   as entries come in; final results land after the scoring phase closes.

## Step 5 — Learn from the result

Whether you win or not, every entry teaches you something:

- Compare your score against the rubric, not against the winner.
- Read the top entries if they are public — they are free lessons in
  what the judges rewarded.
- Keep the repo. A tidy public repo is a portfolio piece even if it did
  not place.

Then pick the next bounty. Most regulars treat it as a loop: enter, ship,
learn, repeat.

## Your first week on make.fun

A realistic plan if you are starting from zero:

- **Day 1:** Create your account through
  [my referral link](https://make.fun/r/6qhstm5a), connect GitHub, and
  browse the board. Bookmark two or three bounties that match your skills.
- **Day 2–3:** Read the rules and rubric of your chosen bounty twice.
  Download the starter kit, run `init`, and get the sample project
  running locally.
- **Day 4–5:** Build the smallest complete version of your entry. Commit
  every working step to your public repo.
- **Day 6:** Run the kit's `check` command until it is green. Fix every
  failing gate while you still have time.
- **Day 7:** Submit, poll the status until it reads `scored`, and pick
  your next bounty while the first one is being judged.

One finished entry a week is a strong pace for a beginner. Speed comes
later; finishing comes first.

## Tips that save first-timers

- **Read the rubric twice.** Once before building, once before submitting.
- **Run the kit checks early.** Do not discover a failing gate at 23:55.
- **Mind the clock.** Deadlines are in UTC; convert them to your local
  time before you plan.
- **Keep files small.** Some kits enforce strict size limits on key files.
- **Do not copy other entries.** Submissions are checked for originality;
  borrowed work gets rejected.
- **Ask questions early.** Competition pages and community channels are
  there for exactly that.

## FAQ

**Do I need to pay anything to enter?**
No. Bounties on make.fun are free to enter — you need an account and a
public GitHub repo.

**Do I need to be an expert developer?**
No. Many bounties are designed for beginners, and the rubric rewards a
finished, working entry more than raw complexity.

**Can I enter more than one bounty at a time?**
Yes. Keep each submission in its own repo and respect each competition's
own rules and deadlines.

**What happens after I submit?**
Your entry passes automated gates (originality, validity, file rules),
then gets scored. Poll your submission status until it says `scored` —
a submission ID alone is not proof you passed the gates.

**How do payouts work?**
Winners are paid from the prize pool to the wallet registered in the
submission repo (`.makefun/owner.json`). Make sure it is correct before
you submit.
